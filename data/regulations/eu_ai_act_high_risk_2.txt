EU AI Act high-risk banking model update — supplementary obligations.

Scope note: this file extends eu_ai_act_high_risk.txt with additional
provider and deployer duties that apply to high-risk AI systems used in
retail and commercial banking, including credit scoring, pricing, fraud
detection, anti-money-laundering triage, customer onboarding, and
workforce decisioning.

Conformity assessment and CE marking.
Before placing a high-risk AI system on the Union market or putting it
into service, the provider must ensure the system undergoes the relevant
conformity assessment procedure, draw up an EU declaration of conformity,
affix the CE marking, and keep the declaration for 10 years at the
disposal of national competent authorities. Substantial modifications to
the system trigger a new conformity assessment.

Quality management system.
Providers of high-risk AI systems must put in place a documented quality
management system covering regulatory compliance strategy, design
control, data management procedures, risk management, post-market
monitoring, incident reporting, communication with authorities and
notified bodies, record-keeping, and resource management. The QMS must
be proportionate to the size of the provider's organisation.

Post-market monitoring plan.
Providers must establish and document a post-market monitoring system
proportionate to the nature of the AI technologies and the risks of the
high-risk AI system. The plan must actively and systematically collect,
document, and analyse relevant data on performance throughout the
lifetime of the system, and enable the provider to evaluate continuous
compliance with the requirements for high-risk AI systems.

Automatic logging and log retention.
High-risk AI systems must technically allow for the automatic recording
of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system. Logging capabilities
must ensure a level of traceability appropriate to the intended purpose,
including recording of the period of each use, the reference database
against which input data has been checked, the input data for which the
search has led to a match, and identification of the natural persons
involved in the verification of results. Logs must be retained by the
provider for a period appropriate to the intended purpose, of at least
six months, unless a longer period is required by applicable Union or
national law.

Transparency and instructions for use.
High-risk AI systems must be accompanied by instructions for use in a
digital or non-digital format, in a language easily understood by
deployers, containing concise, complete, correct, and clear information
that is relevant, accessible, and comprehensible to deployers. The
instructions must include the identity and contact details of the
provider, the characteristics, capabilities, and limitations of
performance of the system, expected accuracy, robustness, and
cybersecurity metrics, foreseeable circumstances that may lead to risks,
human oversight measures, the expected lifetime of the system, and any
maintenance and care measures.

Deployer duties for banks operating high-risk AI systems.
Deployers must take appropriate technical and organisational measures
to ensure they use high-risk AI systems in accordance with the
instructions for use. Deployers must assign human oversight to natural
persons with the necessary competence, training, authority, and
support. Where deployers have control over input data, they must ensure
that input data is relevant and sufficiently representative in view of
the intended purpose of the high-risk AI system. Deployers must monitor
the operation of the high-risk AI system on the basis of the
instructions for use and, where relevant, inform providers of any
suspected serious incident or malfunctioning.

Fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA) for banking deployers.
Before deploying a high-risk AI system used to evaluate creditworthiness
of natural persons, to establish credit scores, or to price or risk-rate
life or health insurance, the deployer must perform a FRIA covering: a
description of the deployer's processes in which the system will be
used; the period of time and frequency of intended use; the categories
of natural persons and groups likely to be affected; the specific risks
of harm likely to have an impact on the affected persons or groups; a
description of human oversight measures; and measures to be taken in
case of materialisation of those risks, including internal governance
and complaint mechanisms.

Data governance duties beyond training data.
Training, validation, and testing datasets must be subject to data
governance and management practices appropriate for the intended purpose
of the high-risk AI system. Those practices must concern in particular:
the relevant design choices; data collection processes and origin of
data; relevant data-preparation processing operations such as
annotation, labelling, cleaning, updating, enrichment, and aggregation;
formulation of assumptions, in particular with respect to information
that the data are supposed to measure and represent; assessment of the
availability, quantity, and suitability of the datasets; examination in
view of possible biases likely to affect health and safety of persons,
have a negative impact on fundamental rights, or lead to discrimination;
appropriate measures to detect, prevent, and mitigate such biases; and
identification of relevant data gaps or shortcomings and how they can be
addressed.

Human oversight in credit and fraud decisioning.
For AI systems used in creditworthiness, pricing, and fraud detection,
human oversight must be designed so that the natural persons overseeing
the system can: fully understand the relevant capacities and limitations
of the high-risk AI system; remain aware of the possible tendency of
automatically relying or over-relying on the output produced (automation
bias); correctly interpret the system's output; decide, in any
particular situation, not to use the high-risk AI system or otherwise
disregard, override, or reverse the output; and intervene in the
operation of the system or interrupt it through a stop function.

Cybersecurity requirements.
Technical solutions to address AI-specific vulnerabilities must include,
where appropriate, measures to prevent, detect, respond to, resolve, and
control attacks trying to manipulate the training dataset (data
poisoning), pre-trained components used in training (model poisoning),
inputs designed to cause the model to make a mistake (adversarial
examples or model evasion), confidentiality attacks, or model flaws
that could lead to harmful decisions.

Record-keeping and evidence retention.
The provider must, for a period ending 10 years after the high-risk AI
system has been placed on the market or put into service, keep at the
disposal of the national competent authorities: the technical
documentation; the documentation concerning the quality management
system; the documentation concerning changes approved by notified
bodies where applicable; the decisions and other documents issued by
notified bodies where applicable; and the EU declaration of conformity.

Interaction with existing financial services law.
For credit institutions regulated under Union financial services law,
the internal governance arrangements and processes required by that law
are deemed to satisfy the QMS obligation for the elements that overlap.
Serious incident reporting obligations under this Regulation apply in
addition to and without prejudice to obligations under Regulation (EU)
No 575/2013 (CRR), Directive 2013/36/EU (CRD IV), and Regulation (EU)
2022/2554 (DORA), and duplicated reporting should be avoided through
coordination with the relevant supervisory authority.

Prohibited practices that banks must not deploy.
Regardless of high-risk classification, banks must not place on the
market, put into service, or use AI systems that: deploy subliminal
techniques beyond a person's consciousness or purposefully manipulative
or deceptive techniques to materially distort behaviour in a way that
causes significant harm; exploit vulnerabilities of a specific group of
persons due to age, disability, or a specific social or economic
situation; evaluate or classify natural persons based on social
behaviour or personality characteristics leading to detrimental or
unfavourable treatment (social scoring); or infer emotions of a natural
person in the workplace, except for medical or safety reasons.

Penalties.
Non-compliance with the prohibitions may attract administrative fines
of up to 35,000,000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 7%
of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial
year, whichever is higher. Non-compliance with obligations applicable
to providers, deployers, importers, distributors, or notified bodies
may attract fines of up to 15,000,000 EUR or, if the offender is an
undertaking, up to 3% of its total worldwide annual turnover, whichever
is higher. Supply of incorrect, incomplete, or misleading information
to notified bodies or competent authorities may attract fines of up to
7,500,000 EUR or 1% of turnover.
